# A History of Method Extension in Change Ringing

## 1. Modern Definition (2023 Framework Version 2)

Before tracing the history, it is useful to state the current position as defined in the Central Council of Church Bell Ringers' *Framework for Method Ringing* (Version 2, published 2023, replacing Version 1 of 2019).[^1]

**Method Extension** is the process by which a method at a lower stage (the *Parent Method*) is used to generate related methods at higher stages (called *Extensions*). Two methods at different stages are *Related Methods* if the higher-stage method can be obtained by applying a recognised *Extension Process* to the lower-stage method.

The Framework recognises three formal Extension Processes:[^2]

- **Extension Process 1 (EP1):** Keeps the lead length constant. Used for principles and methods with Little hunt bells. Works by selecting a *Mode* m and expanding all places above place m by 2 for each two-stage increment. The Extension Construction is `EP1-m` (e.g. `EP1-4` for Little Bob Minor).

- **Extension Process 2 (EP2):** Extends a single-hunt plain method to a twin-hunt method by adding a second hunt bell. Both hunt bells in the Extension hunt one place further from the lead than in the Parent Method, and they Course. The stage increases by one. Construction is simply `EP2`. (E.g. St Simon's Bob Doubles to St Simon's Bob Minor.)

- **Extension Process 3 (EP3):** The most widely used process. Copies and inserts sections of place notation, expanding places above a chosen Mode. Above the treble, work is copied backward from the half-lead; below the treble, forward from the lead-head. The Extension Construction is `EP3-[mode above][sections above]/[mode below][sections below]` (e.g. `EP3-1AB/1DE` for Cambridge Surprise, `EP3-3ABCD/3FGHI` for Superlative Surprise).[^2]

An **Extension Path** is established when two Related Methods with the same Class Descriptor are given the same name. Once established, new Extensions on the same path must take that name, and methods off the path must not.[^1]

Extension Processes may not be combined (only one process per method per stage), though different processes may extend the same Parent Method to different stages. Bands wishing to use processes not in the Council's collection may submit them for review.[^2]

## 2. Pre-History: Before the Ringing World (pre-1911)

The concept that some methods were naturally "the same method on different numbers of bells" long predates formal regulation. Plain Bob, Grandsire, Kent Treble Bob, and Oxford Treble Bob had been rung on multiple stages for centuries. By the late 19th century, methods like Stedman and Double Norwich Court Bob had accepted Royal and Maximus versions. Shipway's Double Norwich Royal was published in the early 19th century and "for a century... has been the accepted extension" despite bearing "no likeness to the Major."[^3]

In 1907, the College Youths rang the first peal of an alleged extension of a Surprise method -- Superlative Surprise Royal, attributed to William Shipway -- and the extension controversy began in earnest.[^4] However, there was no systematic theory. When ringers began extending Surprise methods, they had no agreed rules. As the *Ringing World* later reflected, "it is remarkable and significant that though each man backs his version with copious argument, the results are almost always different."[^5]

---

## 3. The Earliest Debates (1911-1914)

The *Ringing World* (RW) was founded in 1911, and method extension appeared in its pages almost immediately:

- **1911:** "The extension of a method to higher numbers" was discussed. A special report appeared under the heading "Extension" on page 332.[^6]
- **1913:** An article described "the extension of Superlative Surprise Major" and noted that "the extension of Surprise methods" were unsettled -- the writer felt it was "not in a position to formulate rules."[^7]
- **1914:** The extension of Cambridge Royal and Maximus was published. The same year saw the first major dispute: the Rev. H. Law James published an extension of Superlative Surprise Major to Royal. This was immediately contested by others who argued "the extension is not acceptable." Law James defended his extension, claiming it was "exactly on the plan" of accepted extensions.[^8]

At the Central Council meeting of 1913, the Rev. H. Law James had moved a resolution asserting that Shipway's method was the true extension of Superlative Surprise Major. "The Council discussed, not the problem, but their ability to judge the subject, and adjourned it for a year, when the motion was ultimately withdrawn, in view of the general feeling that the Council was not competent to express an opinion."[^9]

## 4. The Great Extension Wars (1921-1928)

The period 1921-1928 saw an unprecedented volume of debate. The *Ringing World* became the primary forum, and the discussions reveal a community struggling to define the very meaning of "extension."

### 4.1 The "Oxford Extension" vs. "Cambridge Extension" Distinction (1921)

In August 1921, the Rev. E. Bankes James explicitly named and defined the two rival extension theories:

> "Double Bob Minor is the correct extension on what I call the Cambridge Extension. But in the Double Oxford extension the treble has been made to pass over two more bells... Consequently fresh work has been introduced into the method. To my mind it is not the same method but we may call it the Oxford Extension; or adopt Mr. Wm. Snowdon's term and call it the extension of stagnation. The difference between the two can best be seen by comparing the work of Double Oxford with the work of Cambridge Surprise. In the one the bells are kept dodging for a longer time in every place, that is the stagnation. In the other the bells reproduce their work at exactly the same length in every place."[^10]

George Baker had already discussed the distinction in earlier correspondence, describing the Oxford approach as producing an "extension of stagnation."[^11] A third variant, the "London Extension," was proposed by William Taylor in September 1921, describing it as the way "Yorkshire Surprise Major may be produced from Cambridge Surprise Minor, giving a method superior to Cambridge Surprise Major."[^12]

### 4.2 J. Armiger Trollope's Four Tests (1921)

In August 1921, J. Armiger Trollope -- who would become the most influential writer on extension theory -- published four tests for correct extension:

> "Here are four [tests], which, together, are quite infallible if you have knowledge enough to apply them. 1. The construction must be the same. This is the one vital test, but does not help us until we know what construction is. 2. The lead-ends must be the same. 3. The work of the bells must be the same and in the same order without any additions or alterations which are not implied in the original. 4. You must be able to lay down a formula in general terms by which, without any other aid, any competent person could write out the method on any number... Don't experiment with complex methods like London. Take a simple method like Kent, and try and see what does and what can happen there."[^13]

This last point -- that a true extension must work for an indefinite number of stages -- would re-emerge 83 years later as a formal requirement in the 2004 amendments.

### 4.3 The London Surprise Controversy (1921)

The extension of London Surprise Major to Royal became a peculiarly contentious issue. Multiple competing extensions were published. The Rev. E. Bankes James published one version; others argued it was not a true extension. Some claimed London "cannot be extended" and had no true extension at all. E.R. Martin called on the Central Council to settle the matter: "There can only be one true extension from Major to Royal or none if a correct extension is impossible. Why are some of the well-known experts who are connected with the Council so strangely silent on this important matter?"[^14]

The *Ringing World* editorialised that:

> "There are a great many ringers to whom, probably, the present discussion... may appear totally uninteresting... But it must be remembered that ringers owe everything to those who spend or have spent their labour in research... Could [the Methods Committee] not consider the laying down of some definite principles of method extension, and so carry the Exercise one stage further out of the confusion which has so long reigned...?"[^3]

### 4.4 The Two Rival Theories (1921)

E.S. Powell provided a systematic analysis of the emerging theoretical landscape, identifying two camps:

> "There are two rival theories as to what the extension of a method to higher numbers ought to be. (a) There are the practical ringers who have mastered a method (say, London) on eight, and desire a 10-bell method to practise, which they can call the same name. They want something ringable, and they expect the work of individual bells to be similar on both numbers. (b) There are the scientists and mathematicians who are seeking the constructional equivalent."[^15]

Powell offered two pragmatic criteria: a true extension should be instinctively recognisable by its inherent excellence, and "if a method will extend from 6 to 8 and on to 10 by the same process you will arrive at an extension to 12. If it will not do so, you may be sure that in 99 cases out of 100 there is a flaw in the reasoning."[^15]

The Rev. H. Law James responded to Powell, arguing that the two theories were not rivals at all:

> "These two theories are not rivals, they are complementary, and we have to take both of them into account... We have, therefore, extended the method according to both of Mr. Powell's so-called rival theories, and so we see that they are not rivals at all, but complementary."[^16]

### 4.5 Bristol Surprise Extension (1922)

The extension of Bristol Surprise became a major battleground. A. York-Bramble (who would contribute to extension theory for three decades) noted that Bristol Royal "has been discovered independently by no less than four of our experts. First by Mr. Trollope, secondly by Mr. F. Dench, thirdly by Mr. George Baker, and, lastly, by Mr. Craven."[^17] But York-Bramble argued that, independent discovery notwithstanding, the method was not the correct extension because "it has not 'associated' leads even! According to our conventions, all correct extensions should have such."[^17]

Bankes James defended his Bristol extension against the charge that it was an Oxford-type extension, arguing: "In Bristol Surprise Major the longest dodge that takes place is one row short of a double dodge. In the extension to Maximus I cannot find any dodging that is longer than that, nor can I find any new style of work that is not contained in the Major."[^18]

### 4.6 J. Armiger Trollope's "The Extension of Methods" (1926-1928)

Trollope's series was the most comprehensive treatment of extension ever attempted. The first article, based on his unpublished manuscript "The Science of Change Ringing," appeared in December 1926 and established his theoretical framework:

> "Of all the outstanding problems of the science of change ringing none has aroused greater interest than that of extension... many men, most of them of ability and knowledge, have set themselves to supply these extensions, but it is remarkable and significant that though each man backs his version with copious argument, the results are almost always different."[^5]

Trollope's key principles included:

1. **Methods are movement, not figures:** "A method is not a collection of figures. There is no more subtle trap that a man can fall into in this matter than to think that a lead of, say, Superlative Major is a collection of figures, and to extend the method to Superlative Royal is to make as near a copy as possible with ten figures of the original eight."[^19]

2. **Extension must be infinite:** "If you have what you call a true extension from 8 to 10 and it will not further extend to 12, 14, 16, etc., then that fact is sufficient proof that the extension is not really a true one, whatever reasons and arguments you may have to think it so."[^5]

3. **Extension is ruled by the same rules as the parent:** "No extension is a true and valid extension unless it can be rung by the same rules as the original. This is the one sure test in the matter, and all arguments and proofs which you can produce are only of use so far as they recommend this."[^20]

4. **The double-is-not-necessarily-double principle:** "Duffield Major is perfectly double; Duffield Royal is not. But Duffield Royal, by the consent of the Exercise, is a true extension. Therefore it is a fallacy to say that because the original is double the extension must be so also."[^20]

5. **Cycles expand in limited ways:** "A hunting course can expand in one way, and one way only. A dodging movement can expand in two ways, and two ways only. This fact provides a sharp, clear distinction between what is a true mathematical extension and what is not. If your extension is produced by expanding the cycles in the only way they can expand, then it is a true extension. If it contains work which is an addition to that of the original, then it is not a true extension."[^21]

### 4.7 The Superlative Stalemate (1927-1928)

The dispute over Superlative Surprise Major's extension came to a head at the Central Council. In 1927, the Council was formally asked to rule on the Maximus extension, but the *Ringing World* pointed out that the Council had already admitted inability to decide in 1913:

> "In 1912, at Newcastle, Mr. James brought forward a motion designed to establish that Shipway's Superlative Royal was the correct extension of the Major. It was adjourned for a year to give the members time to consider the question, and at Winchester it was withdrawn after an amendment had been suggested that the Council did not feel competent to decide the matter. It is hardly possible, we think, for a large gathering such as will be present... to sit in judgment on such a contentious matter."[^22]

Trollope himself attempted a compromise, proposing that the Ipswich Maximus method be called "Ipswich Superlative Maximus" and Shipway's method be "Shipway's Superlative Royal" -- "but I was not surprised to find that the adherents of those methods were not prepared to accept the suggestion."[^19] As Trollope noted, "the question is nearly always complicated by feelings and prejudices which really have nothing to do with the matter."[^19]

The *Ringing World* concluded in November 1927 that "at the present time method extension is in a state of chaos for the ordinary student. And how can it be otherwise when the experts -- men to whom the Exercise ought to be able to look for guidance -- are divided on the very bedrock of the question?"[^23]

## 5. The Consolidation Period (1928-1949)

### 5.1 The 1930s

After the intense debates of the 1920s, the 1930s saw continuing discussion but at lower intensity. A 1931 editorial noted that "such extension as do conform to them will be officially recognised," indicating the Council was moving toward formal rules.[^24] In 1933, "a new extension of Cambridge" was published, attributed to the late Mr. A. Davies.[^25] In 1934, the Methods Committee considered proposed extensions of Bristol Major to Royal and Maximus and of Double Norwich. George Baker continued to defend Arthur Craven's Bristol extension in detail, while noting that Bankes James had in 1922 described them as "Oxford extensions, an old, old cry that had long before been applied to... Double Norwich."[^26]

### 5.2 The 1940s and the Push for Formal Rules

The late 1940s saw a new generation of theorists enter the field:

**1941:** An article on "The Standard Methods" identified Cambridge Surprise as uniquely extensible among complex methods: "it is one of the very few methods of which the work on all numbers can be described in a short comprehensive statement, and therefore, if physical conditions allowed, it could be rung on a hundred bells or a thousand."[^27]

**1948:** W. Taylor began publishing detailed structural analyses, arguing that extension should be about "opening up the basic structure" of a method.[^28] An anonymous letter challenged the then-prevalent idea that "a method to be the same must have the same lead-head from Minor upwards," calling this "a false idea" and demonstrating with pivot bell variations in Double Norwich.[^29]

**1949:** This was the pivotal year. At the Central Council meeting, the Universities Association submitted a motion: "That this Council undertakes to review the principles of method structure, with the purpose of adopting criteria for the recognition of the extended form of a basic method." The motion was referred to the Methods Committee.[^30]

W. Taylor published an influential series applying what he called "Proximate Extension" theory. In response, A. York-Bramble developed a competing analytical framework, which he called "Grid Analysis." The debate between Taylor and York-Bramble occupied many pages, with York-Bramble challenging Taylor's terminology and Taylor demonstrating why, under Grid rules, "London Major cannot be produced from Minor in the same way that Cambridge Major is produced from Minor." The *Ringing World* itself noted that "probably more ink has been spilt over method extension than over any other matter with which the Council has been concerned."[^31]

## 6. The First Formal Reports (1950-1953)

### 6.1 The 1950 Preliminary Report

Signed by K. W. H. Felstead for the Methods Committee, the 1950 report was titled "The Extension of Even-bell Methods to Higher Even Numbers." Its opening sentence set the tone: "There can be no 'mass production' system for the extension of methods since there is no system of extension which can be rigidly applied to all methods."[^32]

The report identified three traditional extension approaches: (a) extension backward toward the lead-head, (b) extension forward toward the half-lead, and (c) extension by insertion of additional work in the centre of the half-lead. The key principle was: "We believe that the objective in extension should be to produce a method acceptable to the practical ringer (not so much the mathematician) as the obvious extended form of the parent."[^32]

### 6.2 The Grid Analysis Framework (1950)

York-Bramble's major theoretical contribution, "MORE HASTE, LESS SPEED," published over two issues in August 1950, laid out a complete analytical framework:

> "Methods may be correctly analysed mathematically, in their basic forms, by a process that may be likened to that employed in examining geometrical figures for the properties of similarity. The process enables the investigator to produce at will the extension of any ringing method. The result is called Theoretic. If the theoretic extension exhibits the requirements of the practical ringer... then it is said to be in the Ideal class of extension. The standard example of this class in Surprise methods is Cambridge."[^33]

York-Bramble distinguished four classes: **Theoretic** (mathematically derived), **Ideal** (theoretic meeting all practical requirements), **Modified** (needing alterations), and **Proximate** (the modified form with fewest alterations). He demonstrated the framework using London Surprise and Wembley as worked examples.

### 6.3 The 1952 Supplementary Report -- Pivot Bells

The 1952 report focused almost exclusively on **pivot bells**. The Committee's key insight was that "the 'likeness' of the blue-line of the extension to that of the parent will be governed by the order in which the leads are joined together. This order is determined by the sequence of pivot bells from parent to extension."[^34]

The report demonstrated that only very few pivot sequences produce a satisfactory blue line. The recommended sequences produced the lead-heads of Plain Bob and Cambridge Surprise on all numbers, "and no others." The Committee recommended that "none but those above be recognised," rejecting many putatively valid mathematical extensions.

At the Council meeting, however, the report was referred back. The debate was extensive. E. A. Barnett famously declared there was "no extension of London to Royal."[^35] Harold Poole advocated "mathematical extension" as the standard. The meeting saw strong disagreement between those who favoured strict mathematical extension and those who wanted practical, ringable extensions. A front-page editorial questioned the entire enterprise in January 1952: "Academic discussions on method extension are doubtless most diverting, but what is the real practical value of this problem child?"[^36]

### 6.4 The Landmark 1953 Report

On **June 26, 1953**, the Central Council formally adopted the "**Report on Extension**" at its meeting in Bournemouth. The vote was recorded with minimal debate: "The meeting then voted on the method extension section of the report, which was carried."[^37]

#### The Theoretical Basis

> "Many hundreds of hours on research were spent by the Methods Committee... The committee's conclusions are based on the theory that correct relationship of place-positions in the extension with those in the parent is the 'first principle' in method extension, which can be ascertained impersonally by the application of a mathematical formula based on certain rules suggested by accepted standard methods of varying types."[^38]

The report noted a significant finding: "Out of about 500 methods extended, over 100 Surprise methods have acceptable extensions in one or more stages. This is contrary to what is generally concluded that correct Surprise extensions are few. The formula should prove a generous one in investigating extensions."[^38]

#### Three Classes

All methods were divided into three classes for extension purposes:[^38]

1. **Expanding:** Internal place positions of both front and back subsections expand laterally from the lead, commencing from where the extension becomes effective. In each successive stage, interior places expand 2 places further from the lead.

2. **Static:** The interior places of front subsections remain identical with those in the parent (though they may be repeated). Back subsections expand as in Expanding methods.

3. **Double:** The formula is rearranged because cross-subsection places and front subsection places are identical to those of the back, but inverted.

#### Section Notation

Every parent method is assigned a standard alphabetical notation. For Expanding and Static methods, front subsections are labelled A, B, C, D, E, F... from the lead-head, and back subsections U, V, W, X, Y, Z (or S, T, U, V... for higher stages). For Double methods, the back uses the same initial letters as the front, inverted. In the extensions, subsections corresponding to those in the parent bear the same letter; where interior places expand, each successive stage is indicated by 1, 2, or 3 against the appropriate letter.

#### Key Features

- **Rule 1:** New subsections are inserted between existing subsections and are "associates" of the subsection they follow. In further extensions, "the new subsection is an associate of the latest new subsection and immediately follows it." Any subsequent subsections "remain counterparts of those in the parent and lower stages."

- **Rule 3:** Where the extension commences at the beginning of a subsection, the cross-subsection place and places above are relative. Where it commences at a cross-subsection place, the places in the subsection below are relative.

- **Rule 6:** For Expanding and Double methods, a front extension may not become effective before a subsection containing contiguous places in 1-2. For Static methods, the extension may not commence from a point prior to a cross-subsection place adjoining the treble. Detailed tables specified which start points were permitted.

- **Rule 10:** The pivot place, if at lead or penultimate position, remains there in all stages. An interior pivot place expands 2 places further from the lead in each stage (Expanding) or remains static (Static).

- **Rule 11:** Every extension bears the standard lead-ends and the method type of the parent. The lead-head "place sequence" is preserved.

The report included a **pivot bell table** showing the permitted sequences. The Committee acknowledged that "the insistence on correct lead-head sequence may cause apparent anomalies" and that the matter would be "kept under review."

#### Grandfathering

> "In view of the impossibility of regularising the position of incorrect extensions which have been rung in the past, except by wholesale renaming, the Committee proposes that these recommendations become operative in respect of any new method extension rung after the date of the adoption of this report and that those previously rung to peals may continue to hold the names under which they were originally rung."[^38]

## 7. Working Under the 1953 Decision (1954-1971)

### 7.1 Immediate Implementation

The 1953 Report was widely distributed as a booklet throughout 1954-1960. The Methods Committee began processing extensions systematically.

However, the report was not without controversy. In 1955, open opposition to the Methods Committee's policies emerged. W. Birmingham urged Associations "to instruct their delegates accordingly" rather than "passively saying 'Yes,' as has happened with Major, Minor, Triples, etc., in the past."[^39] Countering this, R. D. St. J. Smith argued for representative democracy: "The fundamental principle of democratic control is that, having elected someone to represent you, you then stand by his or her actions on your behalf."[^40]

A 1957 letter traced the first major extension controversy to 1907: "Method Extension occurred in 1907 after the College Youths had rung the first peal of Superlative Surprise Royal. It ran as follows: --If Stepney Ten Bell Society can ring what they call Superlative Royal, the College Youths can ring what they call Superlative Maximus."[^4]

### 7.2 The Durham Surprise Dispute (1963-1964)

The early 1960s saw one of the last major public disputes about a specific extension, this time over Durham Surprise Minor. The dispute illustrated continuing tensions between the strict application of the 1953 rules and what ringers found musically acceptable.

John M. Jelley challenged the Committee directly, producing parallel tables showing York and Durham from Minor to Maximus and arguing that both follow the same extension pattern: "A babe in arms could see the obvious connection between these methods. Why is it that the Methods Committee can't?"[^41]

C. H. Kippin, defending the Committee, explained the technical reason: "Mr. Jelley's figures... introduce a front subsection (14--14) which does not appear in the parent Minor and cannot by any degree of imagination be interpreted as an 'extension.'"[^42]

John R. Mayne provided a detailed technical explanation of why Durham failed under the formula, showing the precise section where the new subsection was introduced.[^43] Stephen Ivin argued for relaxing the rules: "The Durham extensions are quite as sound as the accepted York family."[^44]

Harry Poyner proposed a more radical solution -- allowing contraction from higher stages to prove extension downward:

> "If it can be established that a method on a higher number of bells contracts to a certain lowest stage, then the higher stages shall be regarded as extensions of the lowest stage, even though they may contain one type of subsection which is missing from the lowest stage... This would recognise that the contraction process is the identical reverse of extension."[^45]

The Committee's final ruling on March 8, 1963 was clear: Durham Major, Royal, and Maximus "are not acceptable extensions of Durham Surprise Minor," though "the Royal and Maximus are acceptable extensions of the Major." A new name would be required.[^46]

### 7.3 Extension by Computer (1968)

A significant milestone was the arrival of computer-aided extension. In 1968, Geoffrey Dodds published a series titled "Method Extension by Computer" in the *Ringing World*:

> "A COMPUTER programme has now been written which removes the labour and inaccuracies which arise from finding extensions of regular Plain and Treble Bob methods by hand, according to the rules in the Central Council Report on Method Extension (1953). The programme requires the parent method name... About half a minute later the stage, pivot bell and place notation of the parent method and all its extensions, if any, are printed out. As an example of the speed, the programme investigated a Surprise Major method (Leatherhead) for all combinations of the five extension patterns below the treble, with the five above the treble, for each stage of Royal and Maximus, with all pivot bell sequences possible. The time taken was 27 seconds."[^47]

Dodds noted the practical impact: "It will be found that there are a surprising number of extensions, in many cases with alternatives. It is to be hoped that one possible extension series will in future be blessed with the name of its parent."[^47]

The series examined specific methods: Canterbury Pleasure Bob, Warkworth Surprise, Hull Surprise, Double Oxford Bob, Belfast Surprise, Kegworth Surprise, and others.

## 8. The 1972 Amendment

The original 1953 Report had been in force for nearly 20 years, and experience had revealed limitations. In 1972, the Central Council adopted a significant amendment, published in *The Ringing World*.[^48]

**Key changes:**

1. **Odd-bell methods** were covered for the first time.
2. **Reorganised Extension Constructions:** Above the treble: PQ, QR, RS, ST, TU. Below the treble (expanding): EAB, EBC, ECD, EDE, EEF, EFG. Below the treble (static): SAB, SBC, SCD, SDE, SEF.
3. **Expanding Places Below the Treble:** New rules addressed seconds-place expanding extensions.
4. **Double Methods:** "The extension of a Double method must itself be Double."
5. **Lead-head Requirement** was made more general.
6. **Extension by Insertion:** The concept of "the successive insertion of new pairs of places" was formalised as the underlying mechanism.

The amendment was integrated into the Council's Decisions and formed the basis of what would later be called **Decision (G)** on Method Extension. An informal meeting was arranged at the Kenilworth Hotel before the Council meeting to discuss the "Joint Committee report on extension."[^49]

Following the 1972 amendments, the computer programme written by Geoffrey Dodds was put into use "in the extension of methods for the Methods Committee."[^50]

## 9. Decision (G) and the 1989 Consolidation

### 9.1 The Problem of Fragmentation

By the 1980s, the rules on method extension had become fragmented across multiple documents. As the 1992 proposals later noted: "the Report and subsequent amendments were referred to by the Council's Decisions but remained separate and... it became increasingly difficult for those interested in the subject to discover the rules."[^30]

A 1989 letter complained: "The current rules were formulated in 1953 and have been changed several times since. Inexplicably, they do not appear at all in the 1978 Central Council Handbook and must be discovered by extensive detective work in 35 years'-worth of back Ringing Worlds... Not surprisingly, hardly anyone seems to understand them."[^51]

In 1988, an additional amendment was adopted covering "the relationship between single and twin-hunt plain methods," formalising the traditional extension of single-hunt plain methods to twin-hunt methods (e.g. Plain Bob Minor to Grandsire Triples).[^30]

### 9.2 The 1989 Decision (G) and Explanatory Note

In 1989, the Council adopted a new **Decision (G)** on Method Extension that consolidated all previous decisions into a single text. Anthony P. Smith, speaking for the Methods Committee, explained that the motion "sought to do two things -- to incorporate the rules on extension among the Council's formal Decisions, and to propose the wording of the new Decision." He emphasised that the proposal "did not introduce any significant changes, nor would its adoption affect names already accepted by the Council."[^52]

The Council debate on the motion included:

- **G. Dodds** (Hertford) noted that the original 1953 report had "been translated into a computer program, which had subsequently produced some rather unexpected extensions."
- **A.J. Cox** (Bristol Univ) criticised the formula for treating "all methods as sequences of four-change blocks... whereas not all methods were constructed in this way: Bristol, for example, consisted of a mixture of six-change and two-change blocks."
- **R. Bailey** thought the requirement for Plain Bob lead-heads was "too constrictive" and proposed deleting it. After his amendment was defeated, "the original motion (modified as suggested by Mr. Cox) was passed by a large majority."[^52]

Peter Niblett prepared a comprehensive explanatory note to accompany Decision (G), published in *The Ringing World* on June 16, 1989.[^53] Using Cambridge Surprise Minor as a fully worked example, he demonstrated the formula step by step -- identifying 3 possible above-treble extensions (BC, CD, DE) and 4 below-treble options (EDE, SEF, SFG, SFG expanded), of which only one combination (BC/EDE) produced a valid method -- Cambridge Surprise Major.

The note also introduced the concept of the **extension path**: "Decision (G) allows some parent methods to have more than one extension, so there could be a Z Royal also contending to be the extension of X. In this case the Decision requires you to consider extension (or contraction) to further stages to see which 'extension path' fits best."[^53]

## 10. The Modes Concept (1992-2004)

### 10.1 The 1992 Proposals -- Eight Propositions

In 1992, the Methods Committee under Anthony P. Smith published a detailed article proposing amendments to Decision (G), aiming to "reach a consensus and so produce a motion which will need little discussion in open Council."[^30] Eight propositions were set out:

1. **Relaxing the "Most Stages" Preference:** Allow extensions working for alternate stages (e.g. to Maximus, Sixteen...) to be accepted even when extensions to all even stages exist.

2. **No New Consecutive Blows:** "The extension of a method must not have a bell making more consecutive blows than are made at the related position in the parent."

3-5. **Removing Asymmetries Above/Below the Treble:** Permit construction CD below the treble; permit construction AB above the treble; permit static extension above the treble.

6. **Extension of Principles:** Two approaches were considered -- treating principles like Little methods (requiring identical internal places), or permitting extension by modes. The latter was favoured.

7. **Extension by Modes:** This was the most significant proposal. Mode-m below the treble would mean places above mth's place expand; mode-m above the treble would mean places below mth's place from the back expand from the back. "This would be a generalization of the formula with the present expanding and static constructions as the special cases mode-1 and mode-infinity respectively." Detailed figures illustrated how notation would work.

8. **Extension in Steps of Four Stages:** Inserting four new sections related to four in the parent. "Adoption of a combination of these propositions would regularise Superlative Surprise Major, Maximus, Sixteen, etc., which extends ABCD above, FGHI below, mode-3."

The proposals also noted that the extension of principles was not yet covered and that the simplest approach (treating them like Little methods) "would regularise the eleven principles which have so far been rung at more than one stage."[^30]

### 10.2 Defending the Need for Rules (1992)

Responding to complaints that the rules were too complex, Roger Bailey argued:

> "Do we need rules about method extension? Geoffrey Harris thinks not... He can't understand the current rules, so he thinks there shouldn't be any. A 'marked resemblance' between parent and extension is then good enough for him. Any marked resemblance? Is London No. 3 Royal an extension of London Major? ... Who will judge? Geoffrey Harris? I hope not. There are two reasons why we want rules for method extension. The first is so we don't waste our time in stupid arguments over methods... The second (and more important) reason for wanting rules is that they help us to design new extensions."[^54]

### 10.3 The Extension of Principles (1995)

In 1995, Anthony P. Smith published "Extension of Principles,"[^55] addressing the gap left since the Caerleon Council meeting of 1993 when J.D. Cheesman "remarked that the Decisions on method extension did not cater for principles, and asked what the committee was planning to do to fill the gap."[^56]

The investigation was extensive:

> "As part of our investigations into the implications of these formulae we produced all possible extensions of rung principles using Formula 1, subject to all the additional restrictions, and which gave a valid principle at alternate higher stages. 86 principles produced 375 extension constructions and the notations for each of these were generated at the first five stages of extension. We are very grateful to Julian Morgan who produced blue-lines of most of these principles to help us with our investigations."[^55]

Four formulae were considered, from most to least restrictive. The Committee recommended Formula 3 -- extension by modes -- with five additional restrictions: symmetric principles remain symmetric; external places remain external; contiguous places remain contiguous; consecutive blows neither increase nor decrease; and within one change, all places static with respect to the lead must be below those static with respect to the lie.

The motion, adopted at Salisbury in 1995, added a new Part C to Decision (G) specifying that for principles: "the length of the division remains constant; places at lead and any further places adjacent to them remain static with respect to the lead; places at lie and any further places adjacent to them remain static with respect to the lie; and either all other places remain static with respect to the lead or all other places remain static with respect to the lie."[^57] It also introduced the reversal requirement: "the extension of the reverse of a principle shall be the reverse of the extension of the principle."

### 10.4 The 2004 Revision

The 2004 "Methods and Method Extension" article (also by Anthony P. Smith) represented the final consolidation before the Framework era.[^58]

**Background:** Decision (G) had last been "amended significantly" at Peterborough in 1992 and at Salisbury in 1995. At Lincoln in 1999, the Committee had reported considering further amendments, but "other work pressures meant little time was available until the past year."[^58]

**Key changes adopted in 2004:**

1. **Extension by Modes Formalised:** "The most significant new construction is extension by modes. A mode is a notional division within a block of changes. Put simply, in a mode-m extension, the places above and below position m may be treated independently. For non-Little methods extension by modes is a generalization of expanding and static extension, for example, if the parent stage is Major, expanding extension is mode-1 and static extension is mode-8."

2. **Extension in Steps of More Than Two:** "by inserting four (or more) new sections related to four sections present in the parent. This provides another way for a Major method to extend straight to Maximus."

3. **Indefinite Number of Stages:** "The amendments mostly increase the range of possible extensions, with the important exception that they are now required to cover an indefinite number of stages. We consider that existing extensions which only work for a single stage are coincidences rather than extensions!"

4. **Superlative Surprise Regularised:** "A combination of these changes regularizes the extension of Superlative Surprise Major to Maximus, Sixteen, etc., which extends ABCD mode-3 above the treble / FGHI mode-3 below the treble."

5. **New Coverage:** Little methods (mode-3 regularising Cawston Little Bob), asymmetric methods, Differential Hunters ("covered by the Decision for the first time"), and the relationship between Differential Hunter lead-heads.

6. **Symmetry and Hunt Bell Requirements:** The requirement for Double methods to be Double was "generalised to a requirement for maintaining the type of symmetry." The requirement to maintain "the same hunt bells" was clarified to "the same number of hunt bells."

At the Council meeting, the debate was notably subdued compared to earlier decades. Robin Woolley spoke against the motion for approximately 12 minutes, arguing "the proposed rules were too restrictive, too difficult to understand without excessive effort and would reduce the number of extensions actually rung." Anthony Smith responded "that there were often several ways to extend methods: the proposed Decisions should help. The proposed changes would not be applied retrospectively. The motion was put to the vote and carried."[^59]

Woolley later wrote in the *Ringing World* that "between 1989 and 1992, the position seems to have been a little confused, but since 1992 until now, no free choice is allowed... The new decision says that no extensions at all are allowed unless they go to indefinite stages. This means that there are many perfectly good extensions which cannot now be rung."[^60]

Anthony Cox, while supporting the indefinite-stages requirement, noted another limitation: "any tables or formulae can never be fully comprehensive and will exclude genuine extensions. In addition the formulae only consider methods on a section-by-section basis and do not recognise that the first members of a series may only become obvious as the series is followed downwards from higher numbers rather than by considering extensions from lower numbers up... As examples, the lower members of the Bristol and Durham families are not recognised as genuine family members."[^61]

## 11. The Framework Era (2019-2023)

### 11.1 Framework for Method Ringing Version 1 (2019)

The Central Council's *Framework for Method Ringing* was issued in 2019, bringing together all the rules relating to method classification, naming, and extension into a single coherent document. Method Extension was Section 8.

### 11.2 Framework Version 2 (2023)

The current *Framework for Method Ringing* (Version 2) represents the most significant reorganisation since 1953. The key innovations are:[^1][^2]

1. **Explicit Named Extension Processes:** Three processes replace the older "extension types": EP1 (lead-length constant), EP2 (adding a second hunt bell), and EP3 (section-copying with mode parameters).

2. **Formal Extension Constructions:** Every extension identified by a construction code (e.g. `EP3-1AB/1DE` for Cambridge).

3. **Extension Paths Formally Codified:** The concept introduced in 1989 is now fully formalised, applying both upward and downward.

4. **No Mixed Processes:** Only one Extension Process per method per stage.

5. **Ringer-Submitted Processes:** The Framework includes a mechanism for bands to submit new Extension Processes for review.

6. **Grandfathering:** All method names recorded before the implementation of extension requirements are preserved. The Framework also provides for cases where a name is unavailable because it was already used historically.

| **Rev. H. Law James** | Produced extensions of Superlative and Bristol Surprise (1914-1922). Named the "Cambridge Extension" vs. "Oxford Extension" distinction (1921). Championed the cycle theory of extension. |

| **George Baker** | Distinguished "Cambridge Extension" from "Oxford Extension" (1921). Published extensions of Double Norwich, Superlative, and many other methods. Introduced "Single Extension" concept using round block progressions. |
| **A. York-Bramble** | Contributor to extension theory for three decades (1922-1955). Developed "Grid Analysis" and the Theoretic/Ideal/Modified/Proximate classification (1950). Engaged in lengthy debate with W. Taylor. |
| **E.S. Powell** | Provided early systematic analysis of the two rival extension theories (1921). Proposed the test that a true extension must work for an indefinite series of stages. |
| **W. Taylor** | Developed "Proximate Extension" theory (1949-1950). Proposed the "London Extension" as a third extension type (1921). |
| **K. W. H. Felstead** | Signed the 1950 and 1952 reports. Established the pivot bell theory that became central to the 1953 Report. |
| **C. K. Lewis** | Convened the Methods Committee that produced the 1953 Report and subsequent amendments through the 1960s-1970s. |
| **Harold J. Poole** | Leading voice on the Methods Committee 1950-1953. Advocated "mathematical extension" as the standard. |
| **Frank T. Blagrove** | Active in the Durham Surprise extension dispute (1963-1964). Contributed to extension rules. |

| **Peter Niblett** | Prepared the 1989 explanatory note on Decision (G) with the definitive worked example of Cambridge Surprise Minor. Served as Methods Committee secretary. |
| **Anthony P. Smith** | Chairman of the Methods Committee through 1992-2004. Authored the 1992 eight propositions, the 1995 "Extension of Principles" paper, and the 2004 "Methods and Method Extension" consolidation. Drove the modes concept from proposal to adoption. |
| **Julian Morgan** | Produced blue lines of 375 extension constructions for the 1995 principles investigation. |
| **Roger Bailey** | Defended the need for formal rules in the 1992 debate, arguing that without them ringers would "waste our time in stupid arguments." |

| 1911 | *The Ringing World* founded. Method extension discussed from the first volume. |
| 1913 | Rev. H. Law James moves Central Council resolution on Superlative extension; adjourned for a year and ultimately withdrawn. |
| 1914 | First major dispute: extension of Superlative Surprise. Law James publishes his extension. Cambridge Royal and Maximus published. |
| August 1921 | Bankes James names "Cambridge Extension" vs. "Oxford Extension." Trollope publishes four tests for correct extension. |
| September 1921 | William Taylor proposes "London Extension" as third type. London Surprise controversy at its height. |
| December 1921 | E.S. Powell systematises the two rival theories. Law James argues they are complementary. |
| 1922 | Bristol Surprise extension debate: Bankes James vs. York-Bramble. Four independent discoverers of same Bristol Royal. Trollope introduces Q-sets to extension theory. |
| December 1926 | Trollope's "The Science of Change Ringing" extract published -- the most comprehensive theoretical treatment. Establishes: methods are movement; extension must be infinite; places are NOT the essential thing. |
| 1927-1928 | Trollope's "The Extension of Methods" series. Council asked to rule on Superlative Maximus. Trollope proposes compromise (rejected). Debate on whether Cambridge Major or Lincolnshire is true extension of Cambridge Minor. |
| 1949 | Universities Association motion: Council undertakes to develop extension criteria. W. Taylor's "Proximate Extension" series. |
| 1950 | York-Bramble's "MORE HASTE, LESS SPEED" -- Grid Analysis and Theoretic/Ideal/Modified/Proximate classification. First formal Methods Committee report by Felstead. |
| 1952 | "Supplementary Report on Method Extension" focusing on pivot bells. Report referred back by Council. E.A. Barnett declares "no extension of London to Royal." |
| **26 June 1953** | **Landmark "Report on Extension" adopted by Central Council at Bournemouth. Section notation (A-Z), 11 rules, three method classes (Expanding/Static/Double), pivot bell table. Grandfathering clause.** |
| 1954-1967 | Working under the 1953 rules. Durham Surprise dispute (1963-1964) -- Committee rules Durham Major not extension of Durham Minor. |
| 1968 | Geoffrey Dodds' "Method Extension by Computer" series -- FORTRAN on IBM 360, 27 seconds to check all extension combinations. |
| 1969 | Decision (E) D.4(b) adopted at St. Albans: extension relationships covering most stages take preference. |
| 1972 | **Major amendment:** odd-bell methods covered, reorganised construction codes, lead-head requirement generalised. |
| 1988 | Amendment covering single-hunt to twin-hunt plain method extension. |
| 1989 | **Decision (G) consolidated.** Peter Niblett's explanatory note with Cambridge Surprise Minor worked example. Bailey amendment defeated; Cox amendment accepted. Motion "passed by a large majority." |
| 1992 | **Eight propositions** by Anthony P. Smith: modes concept, steps of four stages, static above treble, AB/CD constructions. "Most stages" changed to "indefinite number of stages." |
| 1993 | J.D. Cheesman requests coverage of principles at Caerleon Council meeting. |
| 1995 | **"Extension of Principles" added as Part C of Decision (G).** Four formulae considered; Formula 3 (modes) adopted. 375 constructions from 86 principles investigated. Julian Morgan's contribution. |
| 2004 | **"Methods and Method Extension" -- modes formalised, indefinite stages required, asymmetric methods and Differential Hunters covered, Superlative Surprise regularised.** Council debate subdued; motion carried against Woolley's opposition. |
| 2019 | **Framework for Method Ringing Version 1** issued. |
| 2023 | **Framework Version 2** with formal Extension Processes (EP1, EP2, EP3). Appendix D with full EP3 specification. |
